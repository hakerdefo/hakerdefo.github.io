Surat in the late nineties was a city of friction. It was all two-stroke exhaust, suffocating heat, and the relentless beat of textile looms and the hum of the diamond polishers. It was no place for a hurricane, but that’s exactly what she was. 
<!--more-->
She had been exiled across an ocean, carrying the ghosts of Miami with her to the dusty gates of Dr. S&SS Gandhi College. She was a collision of worlds. Raised among Cubans and Caribbeans, her English possessed a breathless, rhythmic cadence that she constantly tried to edit, though a stray *“mira”* or *“dios mío”* would always bleed through when her brain outpaced her mouth. And her brain was always outpacing everything. To this day, I have never encountered a more terrifyingly brilliant piece of biological machinery. 

I loved her. And because I loved her, I stepped willingly into her orbit, inheriting everything she brought with her, from noisy rock music to the quiet, underground connections for nose candy and china white. 

We were engineering students, but our true discipline was thermodynamics. We just didn’t know it yet. We had built a perfectly closed system in her flat near the college, governed by strict, unyielding laws. Ten in the morning: round one. Two in the afternoon: round two. It was our forced order. A ritualistic, low-entropy state we maintained to stave off the chaos of our own unspooling lives. 

But no closed system stays perfect forever. The universe always demands its cut. 

It was a Tuesday, and we were losing our fight against the clock. The withdrawals were humming in our bones as we practically sprinted away from campus, our minds deadlocked on the foil waiting on her nightstand. We were entirely consumed by the gravity of the fix. 

Then, a junior girl stepped in front of us. She was clutching a textbook like a shield, looking at her with wide, desperate eyes. *“Didi, I don’t get it. What is the Second Law actually trying to say?”*

Every nerve in my body screamed to shove her into the dust. We didn't have time for physics; we were actively decaying. But she stopped. The frantic, junk-sick energy drained out of her, instantly replaced by the terrifying high beam of her intellect. 

She grabbed the junior by the shoulder, steering her toward a row of parked Yamahas and Hondas. She hopped onto a vinyl seat, unzipped her bag, and fished out a foil-wrapped bar of imported dark chocolate. In 90s Surat, it was like pulling a gold ingot out of a backpack. She snapped off a bitter square, placed it on her tongue, and pointed the jagged edge of the bar at the junior girl.

“*Mira, chica*, forget the equations,” she said, her hands moving in rapid, fluid arcs. “The Second Law is the tragedy of existence. In any isolated system, entropy always increases. People call it a mess, but it’s not about dirt. It’s about probability. You take a perfectly ordered deck of cards and throw it at the sky. What are the mathematical odds it lands in the exact same perfect stack? Zero, *verdad*? There is only one way to be perfect. There are infinite ways to be broken.”

The sweat was pooling on my collar, my bones humming with that sickening, hollow ache. She didn't care. For her, time had stopped. 

“It is the reason time only flows one way,” she said, her voice dropping into a conspiratorial whisper. “You knock a glass of water off a table, it shatters. Energy spreads out. You will never, ever see the universe run in reverse. You will never see those shards absorb heat from the floor and fuse back into a perfect glass. The universe demands that energy scatter.” 

She leaned in, tapping the junior's textbook. “That scattering? That’s uncertainty. Pure, mathematical randomness. And the human brain is terrified of it. It is absolute cruelty not knowing if the next moment brings a shattered glass or a shattered life.” 

She gestured with the chocolate toward the city skyline. “That’s why humans invented gods, *loco*. It’s a thermodynamic defense mechanism. We couldn't handle a universe where everything we love is mathematically guaranteed to decay, so we created a loophole. We invented heaven, a zero-entropy state. Nothing rots. Nothing changes. We tell ourselves that if we follow the rules, if we do good, karma will protect us from the wrath of randomness. It’s a desperate, exhausting illusion. Every prayer, every ritual, it’s just our brains burning energy to impose predictability on a physical universe that is designed to blindside us.” 

She held junior girl's gaze for a long moment, letting the sheer, crushing weight of reality settle over her. Then she smiled, gently pressed the rest of the dark chocolate into her hands, and hopped off the bike. 

The lecture was over. The withdrawals rushed back in. We left the junior staring at her hands like she was holding a live grenade, and hurried to the flat. 

We chased the dragon. We passed the mirror. We used her own brilliant physics to absolve ourselves. *Why fight it?* We thought. If the universe is barreling toward maximum disorder, why shouldn't we? We were just accelerating our own heat death, taking the express train to the dark, silent equilibrium of the void. 

And eventually, she was right. Entropy won. 

But it didn't claim us; it only claimed our closed system. By 2006, the relentless, grinding chaos of our orbit finally broke us apart. The flat was emptied. The relationship shattered. She flew back across the ocean to Miami, and the day we parted ways, I got clean. It took another decade, but with her first pregnancy, she finally did the same. 

Today, she is alive and thriving, running a prosperous business, and raising two kids. The brilliant, tragic hurricane from Miami finally found her anchor. We survived the void. But the shared universe we built together in that flat? The physical reality of those drug-fueled days in Surat? That succumbed to the Second Law. It scattered. 

But sitting on that motorcycle, handing out bitter chocolate, she forgot to teach that junior girl the First Law of Thermodynamics: *Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transformed.*

The physical structure of *that life* was broken down by time and chaos, but the energy of it was never lost. The kinetic force of her hands waving in the humid air, the acoustic waves of her Spanglish slipping through her teeth, the electrostatic spark of her genius, and the raw, desperate voltage of our shared love: none of it vanished. 

It simply changed mediums. It cascaded through the humid Surat air, entered my eyes and my ears, and physically rewired the synapses in my brain. The universe is undeniably rigged toward decay. Everything physical breaks. Our shared world ended. But energy is eternal. It just transforms. 

Into a ghost of who we used to be. Into a memory.
